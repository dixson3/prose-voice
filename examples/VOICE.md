# Voice

A sample voice for persuasive and explanatory essays about how systems and everyday work operate. Adapt these preferences to the author. The intended reader is described in [AUDIENCE.md](AUDIENCE.md); optional visual preferences are in [ILLUSTRATIONS.md](ILLUSTRATIONS.md).

## How the author argues (patterns to preserve)

- State the main claim early, then explain the mechanism and show the evidence. For an explanation, identify what the reader will understand without forcing a recommendation.
- Distinguish observation, inference and recommendation. A plausible account is not a measured result; make the uncertainty visible where it affects the conclusion.
- State conditions with the claim: “A shared queue can reduce duplicate work when every request has a clear owner.” Preserve the condition when shortening the sentence.
- Use a concrete example before extending a point to a broader case. Mark invented examples as hypothetical rather than giving them the authority of reported experience.
- Treat a counterargument in terms its proponents would recognize. Explain the remaining disagreement without attributing motives.

## Register / mechanics

- Write in plain, conversational prose suitable for a thoughtful professional reader.
- Prefer active voice when the actor is known. Use passive voice when the actor is unknown or the outcome matters more; do not invent an actor to satisfy a style preference.
- Use contractions where they sound natural. Use first person only for an attributed view or experience that the author has actually supplied.
- Use sentence-case headings. Prefer paragraphs for connected reasoning and lists for steps, criteria or genuinely parallel alternatives.
- Give numbers their units, scope and relevant time period. Match precision to the evidence.

## Readability (flow & density)

- Review sentences longer than about 30 words for avoidable clause stacking. This is a review threshold, not a requirement to split every long sentence.
- Keep most paragraphs focused on one claim and its explanation. Split a paragraph when it changes question, time frame or level of detail.
- Define an unfamiliar term at first use and show it in context. Avoid introducing several new abbreviations in one paragraph.
- Vary sentence length naturally. Keep a necessary qualification close to the claim it limits.
- After a dense explanation, give a small worked example or concrete consequence.

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
| :-- | :-- |
| State the claim directly. | Announce what the section is about to say. |
| Name the mechanism and its limits. | Substitute a broad promise for an explanation. |
| Use a specific example with a stated scope. | Present an invented anecdote as observed evidence. |
| Describe what changes for the reader. | Rely on fashionable terminology to imply value. |
| End when the reasoning is complete. | Add a summary that merely repeats every point. |

## Generative-cadence tells (AI slop)

- Repeated “not this, but that” constructions: state the positive claim unless the distinction itself needs explaining.
- Lists forced into three items: use the number of items the evidence supports.
- Repeated paragraph openings or identical sentence rhythms: revise for the actual relationship between the ideas.
- Stacked parenthetical asides: move a necessary qualification into a clear sentence.
- Inflated conclusions: replace claims of transformation with the specific effect supported by the essay, or omit the claim.

## Forms (optional)

- A short essay should develop one central question with enough evidence to answer it.
- A slide narrative should use titles that state the point and keep supporting detail in speaker notes. Draft both in Markdown before export.
- A longer essay may use section headings and brief transitions to connect distinct parts of the argument without repeatedly announcing the structure.
